# Relational Reality and the Missing Foundation

**Source** — "Reality Has No Foundation — Carlo Rovelli", `@CosmicSkeptic`, 1:38:59.
Transcript: `transcript.md`.

An interview with the founder of relational quantum mechanics, promoting a book. The physics and history
are accurate; the metaphysical conclusion does not follow from the arguments given, and the gap between
them is the substance of this summary.

## The measurement problem

Quantum mechanics supplies two dynamical rules that do not obviously fit together: **unitary evolution**,
smooth and deterministic; and the **Born rule**, which yields an outcome with a probability from the
squared amplitude and updates the state. If the theory is universal — and no boundary has ever been found —
then measurement is itself a physical process and should be described by unitary evolution, which does not
single out an outcome.

| Response | Move | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Add structure** (de Broglie–Bohm) | Additional variables; outcome determined but unknowable | Explicitly unobservable variables; non-locality |
| **Add worlds** (Everett) | All outcomes occur; the observer branches | Ontological proliferation; probability's status |
| **Deny the question** (Copenhagen) | Predicts outcomes; does not describe reality | Incomplete by construction; where is the cut? |
| **Deny observer-independence** (RQM) | States are always relative to a system | Must give up a single global description |

The source's "add versus subtract" framing is a good pedagogical cut and correctly locates relationalism
on the subtractive side.

## What relationalism claims

Relational Quantum Mechanics (Rovelli 1996): states are relative to another system; facts are relative to
the measuring system; there is no privileged observer, and no consciousness is required; the theory is
universal.

The distinction from QBism matters and the source draws it correctly. QBism locates relativity in the
*agent's* beliefs and bets and requires an agent; RQM locates it in the *physical relation* between systems
and is fully naturalistic. Conflating them is the most common misreading, and the source is emphatic that
this has "nothing to do with human, nothing to do with consciousness."

The universality argument does real work: if quantum mechanics is universal then observers are quantum
systems, and the measurement problem cannot be dissolved by putting observers outside the theory. That is
the pressure that generates RQM, and the source's route to it is unusually clear. The Earth-is-round
analogy is precise — a truth does not become false at a scale, it becomes *irrelevant* — and the
distinction between false and negligible is exactly what the "quantum world down there" picture obscures.

The limit is that universality is a methodological commitment supported by the absence of a boundary, not
a proof. Decoherence explains why interference is unobservable at macroscopic scales without explaining
why a single outcome occurs.

## The arguments, and what they establish

**The train and the coffee cup.** On a train the cup is at rest relative to the train and moving relative
to the Earth; "what is its real velocity?" is empty. The analogy's strength is that it is uncontroversial —
nobody thinks a relativist about velocity is denying that things move.

The disanalogy the source does not address: velocity is relational in classical mechanics **because of a
symmetry**. The laws are invariant under Galilean boosts, so no frame is privileged — a theorem, and it
comes with a conserved structure. Quantum states are not related to observers by a symmetry group in the
same way; there is no transformation taking one observer's state assignment to another's in general, which
is why RQM has to *postulate* relative facts rather than derive them.

The better classical precedent, available and unused, is **simultaneity** in special relativity: there,
"what is simultaneous with what" has no frame-independent answer, and the loss is not a loss of reality but
the discovery that the concept was frame-relative all along. That is structurally much closer to what RQM
proposes for "state."

**Superposition and the person.** If quantum mechanics is universal, a person can be in superposition; but
no one experiences being in superposition. The source's answer is the best move in the interview: it means
that someone interacting with that person might see interference effects that would not occur if the
person were in one place or the other. So superposition is not a property of the person but of the person
relative to an interactor.

That converts a metaphysical puzzle into an experimental protocol, which is operationalism in the best
sense. But the scenario described **is Wigner's friend**, and it is the canonical hard case for RQM:

```
Friend (F) measures spin of S  →  F records "up"
Wigner (W) measures {F, S}     →  W finds the joint system in a superposition
```

Under RQM both are correct. The question is whether "the outcome occurred" and "the outcome did not occur"
can both be true without contradiction. The source describes the setup with precision at 28:10 and moves
on — never names it, never states the objection, never gives the reply. For a position whose entire novelty
is that facts are relative, the case where two observers have incompatible facts is the central test case.

**The chess analogy proves too much.** The rules of chess fix every possible game; yet a seven-year-old who
knows the rules knows nothing about chess; therefore knowing the fundamental physical rules has nothing to
do with knowing reality.

In chess **the rules are complete** — every fact about every game supervenes on them. The reason
rules-knowledge is not playing-knowledge is computational intractability. That is an epistemic limitation,
not evidence that chess lacks a foundation. **The analogy is therefore a worked example of a domain that
does have a complete foundation, where the foundation is simply not the useful thing.** It establishes the
opposite of what it is deployed for.

**The bicycle and the mechanic.** The bicycle obeys the Standard Model; when it breaks you take it to a
mechanic, not a Nobel laureate at CERN. "Physics is badly overrated."

The mechanic's knowledge is **implemented by** the physics. He knows the level of description that is
useful for bicycles; he does not know a level the physics does not underwrite. The example shows that
usefulness is not correlated with fundamentality — true and important — and does not show that there is no
fundamental level.

**The glass of water.** A glass can be described as an object with mass and velocity; as chemistry; as
containing biology; as something you drink because you are thirsty; as an economic object bottled in
Italy; as atoms; as quantum field theory; as quantum gravity. "What is the ultimate glass of water beyond
all these ways of describing it? … The glass of water is all these ways of describing it, and none of it is
more fundamental than the other."

This is the best argument in the interview. The "bottled in Italy" and "because I am thirsty" entries do
real work, because they show the list is not a hierarchy of scales but includes purposes and institutions
that no physical description captures.

But "none is more fundamental" is ambiguous:

| Reading | Content | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **(i)** No description is privileged for all purposes | Each answers a different question | **True and important** |
| **(ii)** No level is the level at which the others are implemented | No asymmetric dependence | **False, or at least unestablished** |

The source needs (ii) and demonstrates (i).

## The correct structure

The descriptions form a **partial order by constraint** — quantum gravity constrains QFT constrains atomic
physics constrains chemistry constrains biology — and the order is asymmetric. But asymmetry of constraint
is not asymmetry of explanatory value: the chemist's account of the glass is not *better* than the
physicist's, it is about a different question.

> **There is a fundamental level in the sense that higher levels are constrained by lower ones. There is no
> fundamental level in the sense of a description that answers every question. The first fact is why
> physics is not optional; the second is why it is not sufficient.**

This reconciles the source with his critics and explains why his argument feels decisive: (i) is so
obviously true that (ii) rides along with it.

## What the position actually is

The thesis that relations are fundamental and objects have no intrinsic natures is **ontic structural
realism** (Ladyman, French), developed from Worrall's epistemic structural realism. The source never names
it, which matters because OSR comes with a known principal objection.

**The Newman problem.** If a theory's content is characterised only up to isomorphism — as a Ramsey
sentence quantifying over all relations — then any collection of objects of the right cardinality
satisfies it. The structural content is trivial: it cannot distinguish this world from any other of the
same size. A position that says there are no intrinsic natures needs some way to specify *which* structure
without smuggling intrinsic properties back in, and that is the unsolved problem in OSR. There are replies
(restricting to modally robust or causally relevant structure) and none is universally accepted.

The point is not that OSR is refuted; it is that the objection is well known, serious, and absent.

The source is independently arriving at OSR from physics rather than from philosophy, and that is a point
in its favour: RQM's route is "quantum mechanics forces relational properties, so structure is
fundamental"; OSR's route is "theory change preserves structure but not ontology, so structure is what is
real." Two independent routes to the same destination is evidence for the destination — a stronger
argument than any of the four given.

**Relative facts and bivalence.** The modern formulation (Di Biagio & Rovelli, "Stable facts, relative
facts," 2020) holds that facts are relative but stable within a perspective, and no contradiction arises
because "contradictory" is defined only within a perspective. The cost is **bivalence**: it is no longer
true that for every proposition P, either P or not-P, absolutely. Whether that is a coherent refinement of
logic or an abandonment of it is the live question.

The source's own rhetoric — "there's no correct answer to who's right about whether the electron is in
superposition" — is precisely the claim that generates the problem.

## Madhyamaka

**Emptiness is not nihilism.** The source says "empty is a technical term for being not that it doesn't
exist but it doesn't exist by itself," which is correct and is the most commonly botched point in Western
appropriations. *Śūnyatā* is the absence of *svabhāta* — own-being, intrinsic nature, independent
existence. It is a claim about how things exist, not whether.

**"Emptiness is empty."** The source reports Nagarjuna's move: everything is empty, so is the fundamental
thing emptiness? No — emptiness is empty. That is *śūnyatāśūnyatā*, and it is the passage that makes the
whole reading work. The doctrine is not a new foundation replacing the old one; it is the refusal of the
foundational move as such. It is also what saves the position from being "everything is relative," because
that would itself be an absolute claim.

**The chariot.** Nagasena asks King Milinda whether the wheel is the chariot, the axle, and so on; the king
says no to each; the chariot is the collection, not any part and not something over and above the parts.
Menander I Soter ruled an Indo-Greek kingdom in the second century BCE, so the *Milindapañha* records a
documented encounter between Greek and Indian philosophy. The source's delight in discovering this is well
founded and the history is correct.

What does not map: the two truths (*saṃvṛti* and *paramārtha*) are not the Western pair appearance/reality,
and the force of Nagarjuna's argument is that the relation between them is not a grounding relation. The
source's mapping — conventional → science, ultimate → the metaphysical foundation he wants to abolish — is
coherent and fruitful and is his, not Nagarjuna's; he flags it as a simplification, which is honest.

What should be added: Madhyamaka is a **soteriological** project. Its purpose is the cessation of
suffering, not the description of reality, and reading it as a metaphysics — even an anti-metaphysics — is
a Western framing that several scholars argue distorts it. The source does note the ethical dimension,
which is more than most physics-adjacent treatments manage.

## The history

Science revolutions are slow: Copernicus to Newton is 144 years. Quantum mechanics dates from 1925;
Heisenberg's *Umdeutung* paper is genuinely opaque; Dirac in Britain and Born and Jordan in Germany
produced the full formalism within months. All correct.

**Kierkegaard and Bohr.** The source attributes the thesis to Hans Halvorson, and initially disbelieved it.
The thesis is real and contested, but the causal chain probably runs through **Harald Høffding**, Bohr's
teacher and a Kierkegaard scholar. The source infers direct influence from proximity ("Kierkegaard was the
only major philosopher in Denmark"), which is not transmission. The substantive point survives even if the
channel is indirect: Bohr's insistence that the observer cannot be eliminated has a philosophical ancestry
in the critique of Hegelian objectivity.

**Schrödinger and the Upanishads.** Correct and well documented. The source's point is not that mysticism
produced physics but that **a philosophical vocabulary can make an otherwise unthinkable move thinkable** —
a better claim than the usual genre.

**Kant and Einstein.** Kant's critical project is a response to Newton (what are space and time, such that
mechanics is possible?); Einstein read the first *Critique* as a teenager. The reciprocal-influence point
is the strongest historical argument in the interview.

**Newton's refusal.** Newton's *hypotheses non fingo* is exactly a refusal to supply a mechanism for
gravity, and it was controversial precisely because it left the account incomplete. The point that
science's power comes from accepting incompleteness is correct and under-appreciated. But it is in tension
with the source's own conclusion: if the virtue is accepting incompleteness while continuing to describe,
the correct attitude is *"we have no complete foundation and should not expect one"* — epistemic humility —
not *"there is no foundation,"* a positive metaphysical claim.

## Morality

Two extremes — absolute truth and absolute good, versus all perspectives equal — and both are "morally
wrong." The alternative is that values are relational but not arbitrary, as the moon's motion is relative
but not arbitrary.

The moon analogy is genuinely good and does real work: it shows that **relationality does not entail
arbitrariness**, which is the fallacy at the root of the "if everything is relative, anything goes" panic.
The moon's trajectory is frame-relative and highly constrained; the structure transfers.

The position described is not moral relativism and it is a mistake to call it that. It is closer to
**ethical naturalism** — Foot's neo-Aristotelianism, Williams's internal reasons, constructivism: norms
grounded in facts about human nature and the requirements of social life, not in a mind-independent order
and not in arbitrary preference. Naming it correctly would have avoided the objection the host raises,
because naturalism has an obvious answer to "doesn't this let anything go?" and relativism does not.

The unaddressed difficulty: if norms are grounded in what human beings are, a sufficient change in what
they are changes the norms. That invites the conservative inference — the norms we have are the ones our
nature supports, so reform is suspect. The source's own example, the decriminalisation of homosexuality, is
a reform explained by moral learning, which requires a notion of moral progress and therefore a standard
independent of current practice — exactly what he has given up. Not fatal, since naturalists have accounts
of progress (greater consistency, reduced arbitrary exclusion), but the section ends before the hard
question.

Raskolnikov is apt and correctly read: the novel argues that moral response is not a conclusion of
reasoning but a constituent of the person, and a rationalisation contradicting it destroys the rationaliser.
That supports his *psychology* and not his *metaethics*.

"Both extremes are morally wrong" is a substantive metaethical claim presented as a neutral observation; it
requires a standard, which he has but does not state.

The relational interpretation is characterised from secondary sources; the primary RQM literature was not
read. The Newman formulation is from memory of the literature and should be checked against Newman (1928),
