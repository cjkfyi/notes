# Looksmaxxing and the Mechanism–Outcome Gap

**Source** — "The Science of Looksmaxxing, with Doc Noc", `@StarTalk` / `@dr.noc`, 1:00:53.
Transcript: `transcript.md`.

A long-form interview with a pharmacologist and immunologist. The guest is unusually disciplined: he
volunteers the limitations of studies including ones favouring his own framing, discloses his funding
model, and promotes nothing. Its value is not any single fact but a discriminator.

## The discriminator

Stated at 50:14: *"There's a difference between improvements in some number on a lab test or a very
compelling mechanism about why something should be important, and showing that it's important in a
clinical outcome."*

This is the surrogate-endpoint fallacy, named and applied:

```
Mechanism M ──(plausible)──► Surrogate S ──(assumed)──► Outcome O
                              ↑ the assumption is where the market lives
```

A mechanism is cheap: a hypothesis, an assay, a plausible pathway. An outcome is expensive:
randomisation, blinding, a comparator, power, years, a pre-specified endpoint. The ratio of mechanism
papers to outcome trials in any field is on the order of hundreds to one, so a market selling on
mechanism always has more material and always has it sooner.

Three failure modes recur, and the episode demonstrates all three: surrogate substitution (the number
improves, the outcome does not), directional inference (the marker falls with age, therefore raising it
is therapeutic), and uncontrolled improvement (the measure improves for reasons unrelated to the
intervention).

The third is the **homeostasis fallacy**, the most common argument in the longevity market: a quantity
declines with age, therefore restoring it reverses ageing. It fails because ageing is not a single
variable being turned down; many age-related changes are adaptive responses. Reduced proliferation
limits oncogenesis; reduced growth signalling limits tumour growth. Restoring a declining quantity can
restore a substrate for pathology.

## The translational gap

"How many times we've cured cancer in mice" is the standard objection to preclinical enthusiasm, and it
is given force by attrition: roughly **10% of drugs entering clinical trials reach market**, and roughly
**5% in oncology**. Animal data are necessary and weak — essentially all successful drugs had animal
evidence, and almost all drugs with animal evidence fail.

The right case to cite is **TGN1412** (March 2006). Six healthy volunteers received a CD28 superagonist
antibody intended to activate regulatory T cells. Preclinical work in non-human primates was reassuring
and the human dose was set far below the animal NOAEL. Within minutes all six developed cytokine storm
with multi-organ failure; all required intensive care, and at least one lost digits to ischaemia.

The mechanism of the failure is the analytically important part. The antibody's target epitope and the
distribution of CD28 on **effector memory T cells** differ between humans and cynomolgus macaques, so the
primate model did not contain the cell population that mediated the toxicity. This is not a dosing error
or a manufacturing error; it is a **model-validity failure**. Sentinel dosing — dose one or two subjects,
wait, escalate — became standard afterwards.

The argument transfers to peptides with unusual directness. A 15-residue peptide such as BPC-157 has no
characterised human receptor, no validated potency assay and no immunogenicity data. Between "this
pathway exists in rats" and "this will heal your tendon" lie receptor homology, expression pattern, dose
translation by body-surface area, metabolic differences, and the TGN1412 lesson: the human cell
population that responds may not be present in the model. The episode's phrase for this is that
biological activity is "not a compliment when it's coming from a biologist."

## Classes of intervention

| Class | Example | Evidence | Route | Regulatory |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Approved peptide drug | semaglutide | E1 — outcome trials | Injection | Prescription |
| Gray-market peptide | BPC-157, TB-500 | E4 — animal only | Self-injected | RUO label; no lawful pathway |
| Dietary supplement | creatine, nicotinamide | E2–E3 narrow; E5 for most claims | Oral | Sold as food |
| Hormone | testosterone | E1 for hypogonadism; E5 for "optimisation" | Injection, gel, pellet | Controlled |
| Behaviour | resistance training, sleep | E1 | — | — |

By evidence and risk jointly the ranking is behaviour > approved drug (indicated) > supplement (narrow
use) > hormone (diagnosed deficiency) > gray-market peptide (never). That ordering inverts the market's,
which puts the injectable at the top.

## The harm taxonomy

The episode's most original contribution, at 49:19: the main harm of supplements is "the absence of other
actions."

| Level | Harm | Detectable? |
|---|---|---|
| H-1 | Direct toxicity | Yes, acutely |
| H-2 | **Opportunity cost** — effort spent on a supplement instead of training | No |
| H-3 | **Risk compensation** — "I take creatine, so I don't need to train as hard" | No |
| H-4 | **Moral licensing** — "I've been good, so I can skip today" | No |
| H-5 | Epistemic — learning to reason from mechanism rather than outcome | No |

H-1 is rare, acute and attributable, which makes it visible and over-weighted. H-2 through H-5 are
common, chronic and unattributable, which makes them invisible and under-weighted. That asymmetry
explains why supplements that are "probably harmless" can be net harmful at population scale.

Under H-1 reasoning the question is "is this product safe?" Under H-2 reasoning it is "what is this
displacing?" The second has no product-level answer and cannot be fixed by quality improvement,
regulation or better manufacturing. **It is the strongest argument in the episode and the one least
likely to be repeated by anyone selling anything.**

## GLP-1 receptor agonists

The only class in this report with a completed Phase III programme and mortality-relevant endpoints.
Large cardiovascular and renal outcome trials in type 2 diabetes plus dedicated obesity trials make this
the strongest evidence base in the entire archive.

The episode calls them "medically revolutionary," predicts large reductions in chronic disease, and
structures the argument well: acknowledge the 20–30 year unknown explicitly, establish what is known now
(weight loss, cardiometabolic risk-factor improvement, the disease burden of excess weight including
dementia), state the inference *as* an inference, then weigh acting on an incomplete record against
waiting 30 years while harm accrues. That is decision theory under uncertainty performed out loud.

Where it is optimistic: that these drugs will prevent chronic disease is a prediction, not a finding.
Whether weight loss achieved pharmacologically delivers the same risk reduction as weight loss achieved
behaviourally is unresolved — the observational associations are confounded, and pharmacological loss
may not carry the same benefit as the fitness and dietary-pattern changes that usually accompany
behavioural loss.

Omitted entirely, and all material: gallstone and pancreatitis signals; delayed gastric emptying with
aspiration risk under anaesthesia; the rodent thyroid C-cell finding underlying the boxed warning;
sarcopenia risk in older patients; and the 2025 concern over **compounded** semaglutide dosing errors.

The lean-mass question is also absent. Roughly 20–40% of weight lost on these agents is lean mass, and
clinical guidance now pairs them with resistance training and adequate protein. Regain after cessation is
substantial — in the STEP-1 extension, participants regained roughly two-thirds of lost weight within a
year of stopping.

## Creatine

Mechanism: creatine phosphate buffers ATP during the first seconds of high-intensity effort;
supplementation increases the size of that store; the effect is roughly 10–20% more work capacity. The
important clause is that **the stimulus still comes from training**. Creatine does not build muscle; it
permits more training, which builds muscle. The effect is conditional on training and vanishes without it.

Dose arithmetic: ~1 g/day endogenous synthesis, ~1 g/day from an omnivorous diet, 5 g/day supplemented —
roughly a threefold increase in availability. Muscle stores saturate around 20 mmol/kg dry mass, beyond
which additional intake is excreted. That is why loading protocols and premium mega-doses are
pharmacologically pointless: **creatine monohydrate and creatine hydrochloride produce equivalent
saturation, and the price difference buys nothing.**

Most nutrients and supplements have saturable kinetics, so the market's incentive runs the other way — a
higher dose justifies a higher price and a more premium positioning. The prediction is that premium
positioning and megadosing will correlate, inversely with benefit.

On cognition the evidence is "much less clear, not particularly compelling as yet." The much-cited study
was n=20, open-label, **no placebo group**, with practice effects acknowledged by the authors. Three
defects, and they are the right three: no control arm, repeated testing of a learnable task, small n. Any
study measuring a learnable task repeatedly without a control will show improvement.

## NAD⁺ and nicotinamide

NAD⁺ is a redox carrier (NAD⁺/NADH) **and** a substrate for consuming enzymes (sirtuins, PARPs, CD38).
These are different roles and the market conflates them: raising NAD⁺ for redox purposes is unnecessary in
a well-fed cell; raising it as a sirtuin substrate is the actual longevity hypothesis.

The core argument is the cleanest instance of the surrogate-endpoint fallacy available:

```
Levels fall with age          [true]
Supplementation raises them   [true]
∴ restoring them helps        [NOT SHOWN]
```

The third step is the entire product.

The one positive trial is **nicotinamide** (the amide, not NMN or NR) in people at high risk of skin
cancer — Chen et al., *NEJM* 2015: 500 mg twice daily for 12 months in 386 high-risk patients produced
~23% reduction in new non-melanoma skin cancers versus placebo. Three points the episode implies but
does not spell out: it is nicotinamide, not the supplements actually marketed; it is chemoprevention in a
selected high-risk population; and the endpoint is a disease outcome, not a biomarker — which is exactly
why it counts.

A single positive trial in a niche population is being used to support a general claim about a different
molecule in a different population for a different endpoint. That is **evidence laundering**: the citation
is real, the inference is not. The same pattern appears with metformin and rapamycin.

On cancer direction, the mechanistic basis for caution is real: many tumours are NAD⁺-dependent, NAMPT is
a validated oncology target, and NAMPT inhibitors are in development as anticancer agents. Raising NAD⁺
systemically is in principle supplying a substrate a tumour may use. The same structural hazard appears in
telomerase activation (Epitalon) and angiogenesis promotion (BPC-157): a mechanism beneficial in one
context and pathogenic in another, with no way for the consumer to know which context they are in.

## Testosterone

The market characterisation is correct: a predatory segment, and not only for older men. The specific
rhetorical move worth isolating is **range-shifting** — accept the reference range, then argue the upper
end is preferable, converting a diagnostic threshold into an optimisation target. It is unfalsifiable in
practice because it never contradicts the lab. The same move appears across the optimisation market
(vitamin D, ferritin, thyroid, IGF-1, ApoB).

Prevalence figures — ~1% of men under 60, ~3% in their 60s, ~5% in their 70s — are plausible but not
verified here; estimates vary substantially with threshold and population.

The strongest empirical point is the base rate. In recruiting for the major trials, ~51,000 men aged 65+
who believed they had symptoms were screened, and roughly one in seven had low testosterone. Among people
who *believe* they have the condition, prevalence is ~14%; in the general population far lower. Therefore
self-diagnosis from symptoms is almost useless, because the symptoms are non-specific and near-universal.

That is the Barnum/Forer effect, correctly applied: describe symptoms that are universal, aversive and
ambiguous in cause, then offer a test interpreted against a shifted range.

What replacement does: improves sexual function, some mood measures, and bone density in men with
confirmed hypogonadism. What it does not: cause hypertrophy. The boundary between **replacement**
(restoring a deficient level) and **supraphysiological use** is the key pharmacological line.

Omitted: the cardiovascular safety controversy, the TRAVERSE findings, and effects on haematocrit, blood
pressure and prostate monitoring.

## Aging physiology

The slope argument is the most useful framing in the episode: you cannot reverse the trends, but you can
influence the slope. That converts an unanswerable question ("can I stop ageing?") into an answerable one
("what is my rate of decline, and can I change it?").

- **Muscle mass** falls 3–8% per decade after 30.
- **VO₂ max** is a strong predictor of all-cause mortality; in 122,007 patients undergoing treadmill
  testing, fitness was inversely associated with mortality across the full range, with effect sizes
  comparable to or larger than many established risk factors.
- **Bone density** falls around menopause.
- **Nonagenarian training**: Fiatarone et al., *JAMA* 1990 — ten frail institutionalised nonagenarians,
  8 weeks of high-intensity resistance training, mean strength increase ~174%, with hypertrophy on
  biopsy. Small and selected, but the point it establishes — that sarcopenia is partly reversible even at
  extreme age — is robust.
- **Dementia**: the Lancet Commission's 2024 update identifies **14 modifiable risk factors**, adding
  untreated vision loss and high LDL cholesterol to the 2020 list of 12, and estimates roughly **45%** of
  cases could be prevented or delayed. The episode's three-cluster grouping (cardiometabolic,
  environmental, social) is a fair simplification.

The most important sentence in the segment is that you can do all these things perfectly and still get
dementia — and the second clause is the actual insight: the failure is not innumeracy but **misapplied
determinism**, treating a probabilistic risk reduction as a guarantee and then experiencing the residual
risk as betrayal.

## Body composition and visceral fat

The visceral/subcutaneous distinction is one of the better-supported claims in the episode. In 40,032 UK
Biobank participants with body MRI, **visceral adipose tissue adjusted for BMI** is associated with
increased type 2 diabetes and coronary artery disease risk, while abdominal subcutaneous fat adjusted for
BMI is largely neutral and gluteofemoral fat is protective. In 44,857 participants, the BMI-adjusted
hazard ratio for incident diabetes per standard deviation was **2.44 for visceral fat**, versus 1.75 for
liver fat and 1.14 (non-significant) for subcutaneous fat.

The episode's specific "6% versus 2%" figures could not be located; the published hazard ratios are
stronger evidence and citable. The direction is not merely correct, it is one of the better-supported
statements in the hour.

## The unaddressed subject

The episode is titled "The Science of Looksmaxxing" and never discusses looksmaxxing. Its content is a
general pharmacology and evidence-hierarchy discussion organised around peptides, creatine, NAD⁺,
testosterone and ageing physiology.

The subculture's characteristic practices are absent: mewing (chronic tongue-posture manipulation,
unsupported after sutural fusion), **bone smashing** (deliberate blunt trauma to induce microfractures —
high risk, no plausible mechanism in fused bone), canthal-tilt and ratio fixation, **DIY filler**
(vascular occlusion, necrosis, blindness), unregulated medical tourism for jaw and limb surgery, and
anabolic use.

Three consequences of the gap. It mis-serves the audience the title attracts, who are more likely to be
contemplating a high-risk practice than a peptide stack. It leaves the most important mechanism
unexamined — the subculture's core pathology is the **measurement apparatus**, the rating scales and
ratio checklists that convert a continuous and largely heritable trait into a score with a rank and
therefore into chronic status anxiety. And it creates a false impression of coverage.

What the episode does say about psychology is good. On **Snapchat dysmorphia**: patients request to look
like a filtered image of themselves, and "the disproportionate expectations from what can be possible in
the real world" are blown out of proportion by what we see on screens. The mechanism is reference-point
shift: the comparison standard moves from an attainable population distribution to an unattainable edited
ideal.

On the poll experiment — respondents asked to coordinate so 95% choose A, ~24% chose B — the observation
is real and the interpretation partly right, but better-established mechanisms explain it more
parsimoniously: **psychological reactance** (a threatened freedom produces behaviour restoring it),
**false uniqueness bias**, **pluralistic ignorance**, and simple **coordination failure** with no
communication channel. The analytic value is not the number but the demonstration that "the data apply to
other people" is a live cognitive default — which is exactly the belief that sustains individual use of
unproven interventions.

## Corrections and provenance

- **"VO₂ max"** is rendered "V2 max"; **"creatine"** as "creatin"; **"'roid rage"** as "Roy Rage".
- **"Big Bang Never Happened Vitamins"** is a **parody** — a running gag, not an advertiser. The real
  sponsor is the mid-roll news aggregator. Scoring the gag as commercial capture would be a material
  error.
- The guest's business model — reader-supported newsletter, no ads, no affiliate deals — is disclosed and
  is a genuine conflict reduction. The residual bias is that retention rewards confidence and
  contrarian framing. That is weaker than affiliate revenue and not zero.
- Affiliate kickbacks in supplements are stated at 30–50%. The structure matters more than the number: an
  affiliate model pays in proportion to purchase conversion, which makes "this specific product" the
  profitable sentence and "the cheap version is fine" the unprofitable one.

## Verification

Several figures are approximately rather than exactly right: oral semaglutide bioavailability is ~0.4–1%,
not ~2%; the UK Biobank 6%/2% pair is unlocated; low-testosterone prevalence by decade is plausible but
unverified. None changes a conclusion. The creatine/cognition study was identified only by description.
