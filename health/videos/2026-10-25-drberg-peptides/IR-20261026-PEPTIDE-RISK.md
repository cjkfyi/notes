# Peptide Claims and Supply-Chain Risk

**Source** — "Everything You Need to Know About Peptides", `@Drberg`, 12:33.
Transcript: `transcript.md`.

A short consumer-facing explainer on peptides, framed around the confusion between GLP-1 receptor
agonists, collagen powder, and BPC-157. The presenter is a chiropractor with a supplement business; the
pharmacology is largely accurate and the regulatory claim is not.

## What a peptide is, and why the word is not a drug class

A peptide is a chain of amino acids. The peptide/protein boundary is conventional, not natural —
roughly 50 residues or ~10 kDa — and exists because short chains behave differently: synthesizable,
sometimes orally survivable, cheap to manufacture.

| Class | Length | Examples | Relevance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Di/tripeptide | 2–3 | GHK-Cu, carnosine | Absorbed intact; oral bioavailability plausible |
| Oligopeptide | 4–20 | BPC-157 (15), Epitalon (4), TB-500 (~7) | The gray-market core; cheap to make, hard to QC |
| Polypeptide | 21–50 | GLP-1 (30), semaglutide (31), sermorelin (29) | Recombinant or complex synthesis; regulated as drugs |
| Protein hydrolysate | variable | Collagen "peptides" | Food ingredient, 2–5 kDa; not a signalling molecule |

The source's "bricks versus blueprint" metaphor is well chosen and correctly separates the two things
the market conflates. **Structural peptides** — collagen hydrolysate — supply raw material; the body
decides what to build, and the risk is nutritional. **Signalling peptides** issue an instruction, and
the risk is regulatory. That distinction predicts the observed risk gradient: collagen is sold as food
and is largely benign; signalling peptides are sold as research chemicals precisely because they cannot
be sold as drugs.

The category error the market exploits is that "peptide" describes *how a molecule is built*, not *what
it does*. Two peptides can differ in receptor, tissue, potency, half-life, route, evidence base and
regulatory status by more than two unrelated small-molecule drugs.

## Half-life as the engineered parameter

For peptides, half-life is the primary engineering problem, because proteases are ubiquitous, the
kidney filters small chains efficiently, and the gut is a proteolytic reactor. Native signalling
peptides are short-lived by design — the signal is meant to be transient.

Native GLP-1(7-36)amide, 30 residues, has a plasma half-life of **1.5–2 minutes**, cleaved by DPP-4 at
Ala8 and by neutral endopeptidase. That is why native GLP-1 cannot be a drug.

| Agent | Modification | Half-life | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exenatide | Gila monster homolog, DPP-4 resistant | ~2.4 h | Twice daily |
| Liraglutide | C16 fatty acid + γGlu spacer → albumin binding | ~13 h | Once daily |
| **Semaglutide** | Aib8 substitution + C18 diacid | **~165 h (~7 days)** | **Once weekly** |
| Dulaglutide | Fc fusion | ~5 days | Once weekly |
| Tirzepatide | GIP/GLP-1 dual agonist + acylation | ~5 days | Once weekly |

The source's "seven straight days" is correct for semaglutide and wrong as a class statement — exenatide
is 2.4 hours.

Semaglutide was also selected for **cAMP-biased signalling with reduced β-arrestin recruitment**, which
limits receptor desensitisation across a seven-day window. The source misses this, and it matters: the
naive objection that a constant signal must desensitise is answered by the engineering.

## Pulsatility

Many endocrine axes are pulsatile by design, and downstream response depends on pulse frequency and
amplitude, not mean concentration. Continuous exposure is not a smoothed version of pulsatile exposure;
it is a different signal.

Growth hormone is released in **6–8 discrete pulses per 24 hours, roughly every 3 hours**, largest at
sleep onset with the first phase of slow-wave sleep. In men, sleep-associated pulses contribute on the
order of 70% of daily output. Between pulses GH is near-undetectable. Secretion is set by the balance
of hypothalamic GHRH (stimulatory) and somatostatin (inhibitory).

The source says "every 90 minutes." The literature's 90-minute figure is the **duration of a secretory
episode**, not the inter-pulse interval (~128–180 minutes). The conflation makes the system sound
roughly twice as active as it is.

Somatopause: GH falls roughly two- to three-fold between ages 30 and 40, alongside reduced slow-wave
sleep. That is real. What does not follow is that restoring youthful GH levels restores youthful
outcomes.

The distinction that matters pharmacologically is that a **secretagogue** acts on the pituitary's own
GHRH receptor, so it amplifies a pulse that somatostatin and IGF-1 feedback can still shut off. The body
retains the control point. Continuous exogenous agonist removes it. Continuous versus pulsatile GH also
produces measurably different hepatic responses — pulsatile preferentially drives IGF-1 and longitudinal
growth.

## The compounds

**Sermorelin** is GHRH(1-29)amide, a GHRH receptor agonist with a half-life of 10–20 minutes. That short
half-life is the pharmacological basis of the "dimmer" claim: administered in a pulse it produces a
pulse, and endogenous feedback remains operative. The source describes this accurately and it is the
clearest passage in the video.

What it omits: sermorelin was approved as a **diagnostic** agent (Geref) for pituitary evaluation, not
for anti-aging or weight loss, and that product was discontinued. Its use for body composition is
off-label at best.

**BPC-157** is a 15-residue peptide (GEPPPGKPADDAGLV) derived from a protein in human gastric juice,
notable for acid stability. Animal literature describes angiogenesis via VEGF receptor and eNOS
pathways, growth-factor upregulation, and accelerated healing in tendon, ligament, muscle and GI mucosa
models. There is **no established human receptor or signalling pathway**, which is why it cannot be
characterised the way a developed drug can.

The human data the source cites — 12 people with knee pain, 200–400 µg intra-articular weekly for 4–8
weeks, 7 of 12 improved; 16 people with BPC-157 plus TB-500, 14 of 16 with pain reduction at 6–12
months — are **case series** and cannot support the inference drawn: no control arm, no blinding, n=12
and 16, unvalidated endpoints, intra-articular administration by a physician, co-administration
confounding the second, and no safety monitoring.

**TB-500** is a fragment of thymosin β4 corresponding to its actin-binding region; it sequesters G-actin
and promotes actin polymerisation, with animal evidence for cell migration and wound healing. No human
RCTs. **GHK-Cu** is glycyl-L-histidyl-lysine complexed with copper(II), a tripeptide with reasonably
consistent in-vitro and animal evidence for fibroblast stimulation and collagen deposition; human
evidence is small cosmetic trials with surrogate endpoints. **Epitalon** is the tetrapeptide AEDG
(Ala-Glu-Asp-Gly), developed by the Khavinson group, claimed to activate telomerase.

On Epitalon the source is unusually careful: it distinguishes the animal result from the human claim,
identifies the single-group problem, and does not assert that the claim is false — only that support is
inadequate. That is the right posture. What it omits is the implication: telomerase is expressed in most
cancers and constitutive activation is a canonical oncogenic step, so a compound claimed to induce
telomerase in humans without independent replication, dose-response characterisation or long-term cancer
surveillance is an unquantified oncogenic hypothesis sold as a supplement.

## The cancer question

Two distinct questions run together. On causation: no human carcinogenicity signal has been established
for any compound discussed, with the partial exception of the GH/IGF-1 axis, where circulating IGF-1 is
associated with prostate, breast and colorectal cancer risk at modest effect sizes, and acromegaly is
associated with increased colorectal neoplasia. Absence of a signal in a population that is not
systematically monitored is weak evidence, so the correct statement is "not established," not "no."

On use in existing cancer, the source's answer — avoid — is defensible on mechanism: angiogenesis
promotion is the wrong direction in a vascularised tumour; IGF-1 is mitogenic and anti-apoptotic;
telomerase activation is a canonical oncogenic step.

The source's own framing, "BPC-157 does not trigger cancer," is over-asserted and is the sentence most
likely to be misremembered. The logically consistent version is: no human signal established, an
angiogenic mechanism supplies a theoretical concern that cannot presently be excluded, therefore avoid
in active or recent malignancy.

## Regulatory status: the material error

The source states that "in February of 2026, the FDA reclassified 14 peptides back to category one,
where you can have a prescription," and that "as of right now, a doctor can give you a prescription for
this specific peptide" — BPC-157.

FDA's live bulk-substance page contains an active **Category 2** table (significant safety risks;
cannot be compounded) and a separate table headed **"Bulk drug substances nominated but withdrawn."**
BPC-157 appears in the second, not Category 1. The entry reads in part:

> Compounded drugs containing BPC-157 may pose risk for immunogenicity for certain routes of
> administration and may have complexities with regard to peptide-related impurities and API
> characterization. FDA has identified no, or only limited, safety-related information for the proposed
> routes of administration.

The same table carries equivalent findings for TB-500, Epitalon, injectable GHK-Cu, CJC-1295, Semax,
MOTS-c, DSIP/emideltide, AOD-9604, dihexa acetate and cathelicidin LL-37.

What happened in 2026: on **22 April** FDA restructured that page, and **twelve peptides moved from
Category 2 into the withdrawn table because the nominators withdrew the nominations.** FDA continued to
publish a safety-risk finding for all twelve. **Withdrawal is not clearance** — it removes a pending
nomination rather than establishing eligibility, and therefore moves a substance further from a lawful
pathway.

The probable error mechanism is two conflations. The active Category 2 table now holds **14** substances,
so "14" reads as the count of things that changed rather than of things that remain. And twelve peptides
did leave Category 2 — into a worse status, inverting the direction of the move.

A further confound: a widely circulated March 2026 story attributed an announcement on 27 February to the
HHS Secretary, stating that roughly 14 peptides would move from Category 2 back to Category 1. At least
one site that published it has since retracted, stating on review that the announcement could not be
verified against any primary source.

Every other error in the video degrades a judgment; this one changes a **category of action**. A viewer
who believes a prescription pathway exists approaches a clinician; one who believes it does not
approaches a gray-market vendor. That is a direct causal path to the harm the video spends its second
half warning about.

## Supply chain

The gray-market chain has five links, none regulated as a drug supply chain: bulk API synthesis (largely
China), bulk distributor, research-chemical vendor, payment and logistics, consumer. At no point does a
regulator verify identity, potency, sterility or endotoxin content.

Failure modes, ordered by expected harm:

| # | Failure | Detection | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| F-1 | Non-sterility | Culture only | Abscess, cellulitis, sepsis |
| F-2 | Endotoxin | LAL assay only; survives 0.22 µm filtration | Fever, hypotension, shock |
| F-3 | Identity error | LC-MS/MS | No effect or unintended effect |
| F-4 | Immunogenicity | Not detectable by consumer | Anti-drug antibodies; possible neutralisation of the endogenous peptide |
| F-5 | Elemental contamination | ICP-MS | Cumulative toxicity |
| F-6 | Injection injury | Clinical | Infection, nerve or vessel injury |
| F-7 | Residual solvents | GC | Variable; some mutagenic |
| F-8 | Dose error | Clinical | Overdose |

FDA's advisory-committee briefing on BPC-157 notes that peptides have an inherent tendency to aggregate,
that aggregates are a risk factor for immunogenicity, that subcutaneous and nasal routes increase
immunogenicity relative to oral, and that an immune response can produce **neutralising antibodies
against the endogenous counterpart** — so the harm is not limited to the injected molecule. No studies
formally investigating BPC-157 immunogenicity were identified.

**What a certificate of analysis proves.** Purity is one question; safety is several.

| Question | Required test | Does HPLC answer it? |
|---|---|---|
| Is the main peak the intended molecule? | LC-MS/MS, sequencing | **No** — a wrong-sequence peptide of similar hydrophobicity co-elutes |
| How much is the intended species? | HPLC-UV | **Yes**, for chromatographically visible material |
| Endotoxins? | LAL / rFC | **No** — LPS has no chromophore at peptide wavelengths |
| Sterile? | Culture | **No** |
| Heavy metals? | ICP-MS | **No** |
| Residual solvents? | GC | **No** |
| Correct counterion and water content? | Karl Fischer, ion chromatography | **No** — so "% purity" can be % of a smaller mass than labelled |
| Stated concentration? | Quantitative assay | **No** — purity ≠ content |

A chromatogram answers one question and is routinely filed as evidence about five.

**The statistics.** The source's 30% wrong-sequence and 10× arsenic figures trace to Janvier et al.,
*Talanta* (2018), a systematic screening of the **ten most frequently seized falsified polypeptide drugs
on the Belgian market** — ~30% incorrect sequences, purity 5–75%, elemental impurities including arsenic
at up to ten times the ICH parenteral limit and all in the more toxic inorganic form, and lead. That is a
real study, and it examined **seized counterfeit pharmaceuticals, not research-peptide vials**. The
generalisation to a 30% base rate for RUO vials is an inference, not a measurement.

The **65% endotoxin failure** figure has no traceable primary source located. Treat as unverified. The
"80–90% of raw material originates overseas" claim is directionally right (China dominates peptide API
supply) and unattributed.

**Synthesis arithmetic.** Solid-phase synthesis adds one residue per cycle at sub-quantitative yield, so
full-length yield ≈ `(efficiency)^n`:

| Per-cycle efficiency | Yield of a 15-mer |
|---|---|
| 0.999 | 98.5% |
| 0.99 | **86.0%** |
| 0.98 | 73.9% |

At a routine 99% coupling efficiency a 15-residue peptide is ~86% full-length before purification, so
~14% is truncated or deletion sequences — many near-isobaric with the target and therefore hard to
separate and invisible to a purity percentage that integrates the main peak. A 30-residue GLP-1 analogue
is worse. Impurities are not inert: work on crude versus purified preparations has shown biological
activity present in a crude preparation disappearing when the same peptide is purified, meaning some
observed "effects" may be attributable to contaminants.

## Corrections

- **"HPC"/"HLC"** is **HPLC**. The entire COA argument depends on this.
- **"serorlin"** is **sermorelin**; **"epalin"** is **Epitalon**; **"GOP1"** is **GLP-1**.
- **"every 90 minutes"** for GH pulses is the pulse *duration*, not the interval (~3 h).
- The **codebook** claim in the adjacent video in this archive — that a codebook "obfuscates" — is wrong
  for the same structural reason: once a primitive achieves its bound, nothing layered above it adds
  security.
